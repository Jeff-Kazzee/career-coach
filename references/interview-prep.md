# Interview Preparation and Practice

## What this reference is for

Prepare for behavioral, technical, case, portfolio, recruiter, panel, and hiring-manager interviews through targeted practice and evidence-based feedback.

## When the agent should read it

Read this in Interview Mode, after an interview is scheduled, when applications reach screening, or when the user wants to build a reusable answer bank.

## The concrete process

### 1. Ground preparation in the actual interview

Collect or infer:

- Employer, role, and saved job description
- Interview date, time zone, medium, and expected duration
- Stage and interviewer roles
- Known format: recruiter, behavioral, technical, case, presentation, portfolio, panel
- User's resume version and submitted materials
- Compensation, schedule, location, accessibility, or work-authorization issues likely to arise

Research the current employer and role with live sources when tools are available. Distinguish employer statements from third-party reports and user impressions.

### 2. Build a competency map

Translate the posting into likely question areas:

| Requirement or risk | Likely question | Best evidence | Missing preparation |
|---|---|---|---|

Prioritize the requirements repeated in the posting, central to the role's work, or weakly evidenced in the application.

### 3. Build a story bank

Use `assets/interview-practice-template.md`. Prepare reusable stories covering:

- Meaningful accomplishment
- Difficult problem
- Mistake or failure and recovery
- Conflict or disagreement
- Team contribution
- Leadership or initiative
- Ambiguous task
- Competing priorities
- Learning something quickly
- Customer, user, or stakeholder communication
- Accessibility, reliability, quality, or ethics when relevant to the role

Stories may come from jobs, projects, freelance work, school, volunteering, open source, communities, or independent work. Label the context accurately.

### 4. Shape answers with STAR or CAR

Use a structure, not a script:

- **Situation/Context:** enough detail to understand the stakes.
- **Task/Challenge:** what needed to change and what you owned.
- **Action:** specific decisions and actions taken by the user; usually the largest part.
- **Result:** verified result, learning, feedback, or artifact.
- **Relevance:** one sentence connecting the story to the target role when useful.

Avoid a long setup that leaves no time for the user's actions. Do not claim a team result as solely the user's result.

### 5. Run one-question mock loops

Do not dump twenty questions and call it practice. Use this loop:

1. Ask one realistic question.
2. Let the user answer without interruption unless they request live coaching.
3. Score the answer.
4. Identify one high-leverage revision.
5. Have the user answer again.
6. Preserve the improved outline, not a memorized speech.

Use this rubric, 0–3 each:

- Relevance to the question
- Clear structure
- Specificity and context
- Ownership of actions
- Evidence or result
- Concision and delivery
- Role relevance
- Truthfulness and consistency

Feedback format:

```markdown
What worked:
- ...

Main weakness:
- ...

Missing detail:
- ...

Stronger outline:
- Context:
- Action:
- Result:
- Relevance:

Retry question:
...
```

### 6. Prepare the opening and core pitch

A “tell me about yourself” answer should usually include:

1. Current professional direction
2. Two or three relevant proof points
3. Why this role is the logical next step

Keep it role-centered. Do not make the user narrate every life event or apologize for a nontraditional path.

For gaps or changes, use a brief bridge:

> I stepped away from [context, only as much as the user wants to disclose]. During that period I [relevant activity or constraint]. I am now targeting [role] and have recently demonstrated [current proof].

Do not pressure disclosure of disability, health, family, or benefits details.

### 7. Prepare technical or work-sample interviews

Use current role-specific evidence rather than a generic coding-interview plan. Determine:

- Expected problem types and tools
- Whether external resources are allowed
- Evaluation criteria
- Time constraints
- Environment setup
- Communication expectations
- Accessibility needs

Practice the full loop: clarify, state assumptions, plan, execute, test, explain tradeoffs, and review. For take-home tasks, clarify ownership, time expectations, confidentiality, and whether the task resembles unpaid production work.

### 8. Prepare a portfolio walkthrough

Use a compact narrative:

- Problem and user
- Constraints and role
- Key decision
- Execution
- Result or evidence
- Limitation or tradeoff
- What the user would improve next

Prepare a two-minute and a ten-minute version. Test every link, demo, file, account, and screen-share path before the interview.

### 9. Prepare questions for the employer

Choose questions that reveal working conditions and success criteria:

- What outcomes would make the first 90 days successful?
- Which priorities compete most often in this role?
- How is work assigned, reviewed, and changed?
- What does useful feedback look like on this team?
- Why is the role open?
- What caused strong people in this role to struggle?
- How are remote or flexible arrangements actually practiced?
- What training, documentation, and support exist?
- How does the organization handle workload spikes?
- What are the next steps and timeline?

Red flags include evasive answers about duties or pay, pressure to accept immediately, disrespect toward former staff, inconsistent interview accounts, unpaid speculative work, illegal questions, or a mismatch between advertised and described conditions.

### 10. Handle salary and logistics

Prepare factual responses for location, schedule, start date, work authorization, and compensation. Do not invent salary ranges. Use current location- and role-specific research when discussing market pay.

A neutral compensation response:

> I would like to understand the full scope and total package. Based on the role as described and current research for [location], I am targeting [verified range]. Is that within the approved range?

The user can choose whether and when to request an interview accommodation. For legal interpretation, refer to qualified resources.

### 11. Run a post-interview review

Within a reasonable time after the interview, record:

- Questions asked
- Strong and weak answers
- Signals about expectations, manager, team, and conditions
- New facts or inconsistencies
- Follow-up commitments
- Thank-you or evidence to send
- Preparation changes for the next round

Separate the user's performance from the employer's behavior. Rejection does not prove the interview was poor; use concrete feedback and repeated patterns.
